# Where these rules come from

These rules are our own summary of how Celeste's, Super Mario's and Ori's levels were designed, plus common difficulty curve and pacing practice. They are not quotes. The numbers for chase speed and margin in finale.md are our own starting guesses; none of these sources gives figures.

- Maddy Thorson, "Level Design Workshop: Designing Celeste", GDC 2017. Covers rooms built around a short story, more than one way through, making impossible routes obviously impossible, safety and pacing, short rooms, and teaching through level design instead of text. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4RlpMhBKNr0
- Casey Jarmes, "Celeste Level Design Case Study" (2020). Covers safe first encounters, warnings before hazards and the gradual introduction of each mechanic. https://caseyjarmes.wordpress.com/2020/09/15/celeste-level-design-case-study/
- Joseph Diamond, "The award-winning level design in Celeste". Covers readable affordances and alternating movement with planning pauses. https://medium.com/@josephdiamond115/the-award-winning-level-design-in-celeste-c2acb315bf79
- Koichi Hayashida (director, Super Mario 3D Land) on kishōtenketsu in Mario level design: introduce, develop, twist, conclude. https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-secret-to-i-mario-i-level-design
- On difficulty curve shapes, including the sawtooth: https://www.abratabia.com/game-design/difficulty-curves.php

## Chapter structure and pacing (structure.md)

- Mark Davies, "Examining Game Pace: How Single-Player Levels Tick", Game Developer (2009). Pace as peaks and troughs; bring intensity down gradually after a hard stretch; breathers let players take stock, but too many kill the sense of progress; set-piece encounters build to a crescendo then drop off. https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/examining-game-pace-how-single-player-levels-tick
- "Celeste (video game)", Wikipedia. Chapters made of single-screen challenges with checkpoints between them, branching paths to optional challenges and collectibles; strawberries and B-sides as optional ways to make the game harder, balanced by Assist Mode. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celeste_(video_game)
- Celeste Wiki, chapter pages. Each chapter is split into a few named parts you can resume from (Forsaken City: Start, Crossing, Chasm; Old Site: Start, Intervention, Awake). https://celestegame.fandom.com/wiki/Chapter_1:_Forsaken_City and https://celestegame.fandom.com/wiki/Chapter_2:_Old_Site?oldid=3188
- Josh Bycer, "Celeste is a Game of Many Ups and Some Downs", Game Wisdom (2018). Restarting at each room keeps deaths cheap; strawberries carry no in-game reward; the critique that some levels outlast their idea (an argument for varied, shorter rooms). https://game-wisdom.com/analysis/celeste
- "What makes collectibles feel meaningful vs padding", MyGameDesign (2025). Collectibles work when each is its own challenge, never required, and few rather than many; Celeste's strawberries as the model. https://mygamedesign-frontend.pages.dev/what-makes-collectibles-feel-meaningful-vs-padding
- Radek Koncewicz, "Super Mario Bros 3 Level Design Lessons", parts 2 and 3. Different stage types back to back for variety (including auto-scrollers); themed world maps; shops, minigames and houses between stages; pipes and detours that keep the hardest stages optional. https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/super-mario-bros-3-level-design-lessons-part-2 and https://significant-bits.com/?p=3432
- Super Mario Wiki, "Break Time! Hurry, Hurry". Super Mario Bros. Wonder's short, optional "Break Time!" side courses, listed apart from each world's main courses. https://www.mariowiki.com/Break_Time!_Hurry,_Hurry

## Chases and escapes (finale.md)

- Celeste Wiki, "Intervention" and "Awake". In Old Site's chase the dark double copies every input as she follows; the next part opens at a campfire with nothing dangerous in it (the release). https://celestegame.fandom.com/wiki/Intervention and https://celestegame.fandom.com/wiki/Awake
- Alexander Pensler, "Enhancing Gameplay with Music in Celeste" (Game Audio Lookout). Music layers build through Old Site, go silent before the confrontation, run at full intensity in the chase, then fade out as it ends, with calm music to close. https://medium.com/game-audio-lookout/enhancing-gameplay-with-music-in-celeste-5da210bbc602
- Maddy Thorson, "Chapter 9 design" (EXOK, 2019). A gate that splits a difficulty spike in two; the game's longest stretch without a checkpoint was built to start at its hardest and end at its easiest. https://exok.com/posts/2019-11-25-chapter-9-design/
- Ori and the Blind Forest, TV Tropes page. Its escapes are long runs without saving, unlike the rest of the game; easy mode adds checkpoints to them; the last escape asks for every ability learned. https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/VideoGame/OriAndTheBlindForest
- "The Making Of Ori and the Blind Forest", Edge (2016). The team tuned difficulty by watching testers, and later acknowledged the Ginso Tree escape stopped some players for good. https://www.pressreader.com/australia/edge/20161013/283407716237477
- Game Informer, review of Ori and the Will of the Wisps (2020). The sequel's escapes still don't save midway but are easy to read and cleared in a few tries; checkpoints placed per challenge make the game more approachable. https://www.gameinformer.com/review/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps/ori-and-the-will-of-the-wisps-review-even-better-than-the
- John Broomhall, "The sounds of Ori and the Blind Forest", MCV (interview with Gareth Coker). The Ginso Tree's music is one long suite of seamless cues that follow the player's progress, tested to keep the music, and the player, moving. https://mcvuk.com/development-news/the-sounds-of-ori-and-the-blind-forest/
- Jared Mitchell, "Creating Horror through Level Design: Tension, Jump Scares, and Chase Sequences", Game Developer (2015). Chases work when the pursuer has been shown before, the way has no dead ends, and choices lead on rather than trap. https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/creating-horror-through-level-design-tension-jump-scares-and-chase-sequences
- Joel Couture, "The art of the chase: Level design and player orientation in Outlast 2", Game Developer. Light and open space lead the player; don't stretch a chase until the tension numbs; sound keeps the pursuer present. https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/the-art-of-the-chase-level-design-and-player-orientation-in-i-outlast-2-i-
- Giant Bomb, "Directed Escape". Funnel the player, give clear direction cues, and end the escape at a checkpoint or a scripted block. https://www.giantbomb.com/directed-escape/3015-5674/
